November: PnL attribution

In March we looked at adjoint algorithmic differentiation and a small scalar tape written in Python, which we used to compute the delta and vega of a Black–Scholes call. In this post, let us look at how to couple AAD with a Monte Carlo simulation and, under a stochastic volatility model, compare two products that are both long a type of volatility: a plain call on the spot, and a call on the realised volatility of the spot. The basic use of AAD is that one backward pass per path gives the sensitivities of each of them to all model inputs, including the parameters of the volatility process. We will see that the two products turn out to have quite different risk profiles, and we will use the AAD risks to check how well a Taylor expansion in the Greeks explains their one-day PnL.

The model. Under the risk-neutral measure, we will assume that spot follows a lognormal diffusion with instantaneous volatility given by the exponential of a mean-reverting Ornstein–Uhlenbeck factor: $$\frac{dS}{S} = r\,dt + v\,dW, \\ v_t = v_0\, \exp\left(u_t - \theta_t\right), \\ du = \kappa(L - u)\,dt + \xi\, dZ, \qquad u_0 = 0,$$ with $dW\,dZ = \rho\, dt$. The term $\theta_t$ is a deterministic function of time, chosen to centre the volatility in some way. Here we take $\theta_t = \tfrac12\mathrm{Var}(u_t)$, so that $\mathbb E(v_t) = v_0$ when $L = 0$; $\theta_t = \mathrm{Var}(u_t)$ would centre the variance instead, $\mathbb E(v_t^2) = v_0^2$, and $\theta_t = 0$ would make $v_0$ the median.

This is essentially a Bergomi one factor model, but the use of the exponential Ornstein–Uhlenbeck volatility goes back at least to Scott [1]. The factor $u$ is Gaussian, and we get that $\mathbb E(v_t) = v_0\, e^{L(1-e^{-\kappa t})}$. When $L = 0$, the volatility is centred on $v_0$ at all times, and $v_0$ multiplies the whole volatility path. The volatility of volatility $\xi$ controls how far the volatility path strays from $v_0$, and $\kappa$ how fast it returns.

The simulation uses a uniform grid of $N$ steps of size $h = T/N$. As it is possible to do so, the factor $u$ is simulated exactly, and log-spot takes Euler steps. To simulate the current market, we will use $S_0 = 100$, $r = 3.50\%$, $T = 1.00$, $v_0 = 40\%$, $\kappa = 2.00$, $L = 0$, $\xi = 80\%$ and $\rho = -50\%$. We will use $N = 50$ steps (so approximately weekly stepping) and $100{,}000$ paths.

Greeks with AAD. For each path, we initialize inputs on the tape as leaves, as we explained in the original post. After the payoff is computed, a backward pass computes the adjoints of the leaves, and their averages over the paths are the pathwise estimates of the first-order Greeks. This is the pathwise method of Broadie and Glasserman [2], and its combination with adjoints was popularised by Giles and Glasserman [3]. It requires the payoff to be Lipschitz in the inputs, which is true for the two calls below, but not for a digital or a barrier, for which one needs alternative methods to make AAD Greeks reliable. Note that the step $h = T/N$ is computed on the tape from $T$, so that Theta comes out of the backward pass as well, although one should be careful about this value when we want to capture the effect of time properly on a portfolio.

Unfortunately, second derivatives cannot be obtained in the same way: we must compute gamma, the spot-volatility cross Greek and the volatility gamma by central differences of the AAD first derivatives, bumping spot and the starting value $u_0$ of the volatility factor below. This costs four extra simulations, but bumping all inputs to get the first- and second-order Greeks would have cost us much more.

One tape for all paths. The tape of the March post records one node per operation, holding a single number, but we can instead let each node hold a numpy array with one value per path, and store its partial derivatives as arrays too. A simulation then records a single tape of about $1{,}200$ nodes, and the backward pass multiplies and adds arrays over all paths at once. The leaves still receive one adjoint per path, so the standard errors and the bumped second-order Greeks are computed as before. A simulation of $100{,}000$ paths takes about $0.15$ seconds on an Apple M5 laptop, including drawing the normals, recording the tape and the backward pass, roughly $400$ times faster than a naive float tape. Much better!

To check that our implementation is sound, we can first check the model collapses to that of Black–Scholes when volatility of volatility is near zero. Thus, the AAD implementation seems to be working fairly well. Let's move on to pricing.

A call on realised volatility. So far, we only talked about Black–Scholes and vanilla European calls, which are liquid instruments quoted in the market (for indices) or, in the case of single stocks, American calls, which are “soft exotics”. The second product that we are interested in is a call on realised volatility, whose underlying “volatility swap” pays a coupon proportional to the difference between the realised volatility of the underlying stock and some pre-agreed strike.

More precisely, if we entered the contract and the deal starts at $t_0 = 0$ and matures at $T_*$, it pays $ N_\sigma\max(\sigma_R(T_*) - K_\sigma, 0)$ at $T_*$, for a notional $N_\sigma$ discussed below, where $$\sigma_R^2(T_*) = \frac 1{{T_*}}\left[{A(\tau) + \sum_{\tau \le t_i < T_*} \log^2\left( \frac{S_{t_{i+1}} }{S_{t_i}}\right)}\right]$$ is the annualised realised variance of the log-spot. We are writing $T_* = \tau + T$ to split time into past and upcoming fixings. For this deal, let us take the volatility strike $K_\sigma = v_0 = 40\%$ (at the money).

The term $A(\tau)$ is the sum of squared log-returns that have already fixed up to time $\tau$ and $\tau$ the time elapsed; of course both are zero at inception, and they are not needed to price a new contract, but they are needed to get its value even one day on: by then one return is fixed and the contract is seasoned. This means that we must put $A$ and $\tau$ on the tape as inputs. A day passing moves $T$ down and $\tau$ up, so the theta of the product is $$\Theta = -\partial V/\partial T + \partial V/\partial \tau.$$

Besides time, three things move during a day: spot, the accrued variance and the volatility. A spot move from $S$ to $S+\delta S$ adds $$ \delta A = \log^2\left(1 + \frac{\delta S}{S}\right) \sim \left(\frac{\delta S}{S}\right)^2 $$ to $A$. For the volatility, the state of the model is the factor $u$, which moves by $\delta u$ during the day, so we also put its starting value $u_0$ on the tape, together with the starting value $\theta_0$ of the normaliser (more on this below).

The PnL expansion of the model. Writing $V_u$, $V_{uu}$, $V_{Su}$ and $V_\theta$ for the derivatives in these starting values, the one-day PnL explain I use is \begin{align*} \delta V &\approx V_S\, \delta S + \tfrac12 V_{SS}\, \delta S^2 \\ &+ V_u\, \delta u + \tfrac12\, V_{uu}\, \delta u^2 + V_{Su}\, \delta S\, \delta u \\ &+ \Theta\, \delta t + \tfrac12 V_\theta\, \xi^2 \delta t + \frac{\partial V}{\partial A}\, \delta A. \end{align*} This is a Taylor expansion to second order in the two random moves $\delta S$ and $\delta u$, which are both of order $\sqrt{\delta t}$, and to first order in the deterministic changes: the time $\delta t$ and the growth $\tfrac12\xi^2\,\delta t$ of the normaliser; terms of order $\delta t^{3/2}$ and higher are dropped. The drift of $u$, $\kappa(L - u_0)\,\delta t$, would also enter at first order in time, but it is zero at our starting point $u_0 = L = 0$. The accrual term is first order in $A$, because $\delta A$, a squared return, is already of second order in $\delta S$, as we computed above. In the scenarios below we must use the exact $\delta A$, since that is how much the input $A$ actually moves. The last term vanishes for the plain call, which of course does not depend on $A$ at all. Carr and Lee [4] survey the pricing and hedging of this and other volatility derivatives.

The centering parameter. The term in $V_\theta$ can be confusing. The normaliser $\theta_t$ is a model parameter, a deterministic function of time, and unlike $u$ it is not a state variable. With our choice it happens to be written in terms of the other parameters, but any centering would do. Putting $\theta_0$ on the tape is the same trick as putting $\tau$ there: one day on, the passage of time has moved the normaliser to $\theta_0 + \theta'(0)\,\delta t$, here about $\tfrac12\xi^2\,\delta t$, and the change in value $\theta'(0)\,V_\theta\,\delta t$ is part of the time decay.

We will therefore use the full theta $$\Theta_{\mathsf{full}} = -\frac{\partial V}{\partial T} + \frac{\partial V}{\partial \tau} + \theta'(0)\, V_\theta, \qquad \theta'(0) = \tfrac12\xi^2.$$ Leaving this term out would bias the PnL explain, because the growth of the normaliser is what keeps the volatility centred: the term $\tfrac12 V_{uu}\,\delta u^2$, whose average is $\tfrac12 V_{uu}\,\xi^2\,\delta t$, describes $u$ moving with the normaliser fixed, so on its own it would make the mean volatility drift upwards.

Vega notional and sizing. The size of a plain call is a number of shares, here one, and its delta says how many shares it currently behaves like. The payoff of a call on realised volatility is a volatility amount, a plain number, so there are no shares to count. Instead, the underlying volatility swap states a notional $N_\sigma$, the amount paid per unit of volatility. The notional plays the role of the number of shares, and the sensitivity to volatility plays the role of the delta.

Volatility and variance swaps are sized in the same way, by a vega notional: the amount paid per volatility point near the strike, see [5], [6]. Since the volatility moves through $u$ in the explain, we measure the sensitivity to volatility by $V_u$, the change in value for a relative move of the instantaneous volatility. The $V_u$ risk of one plain call is $6.62$, and that of the realised-volatility call is $0.0950$ per unit of notional. Reading the Greeks. The two positions match on $V_u$ but not exactly on $V_{v_0}$, with $372.4$ against $396.5$. The two Greeks measure different moves: $v_0$ impacts the whole volatility path, while a shock to $u_0$ decays at rate $\kappa$, with a half-life of about $4$ months. Note that at inception, the volatility call has no exposure to spot, but it still makes money on large spot moves, through the accrual term: the role of gamma is played by $\partial V/\partial A$.

Both products must pay theta for their convexity, and, as in the July post, the pricing equation explains exactly how this works. The pricing PDE of this model on its state variables $(S,u,A)$ reads: $$ -\Theta_{\mathsf{full}} = rS\,V_S + \tfrac12 S^2 V_{SS} v_0^2 + V_A v_0^2 + \tfrac12 V_{uu}\,\xi^2 + \rho S v_0 \xi\, V_{Su} - rV. $$ Each Greek is paired with a break-even level, an entry of the instantaneous covariance matrix of the state variables: the spot volatility $v_0 = 40\%$ for gamma and for the accrual, the volatility of volatility $\xi = 80\%$ for the convexity in $u$, and the covariance $\rho v_0 \xi = -16\%$ for the cross term. The terms add up to the full theta within $1\%$ and $2\%$, so the model can explain its PnL very well for both products. This should not come as a surprise, as we are explaining the PnL using “raw” model parameters, but the real test comes with explaining the PnL with instruments we can hedge withs; more on this in some later post. For the plain calls, the gap is within one standard error of the Monte Carlo. For the realised-volatility call, the gap comes from grid bias: once we get to 200 steps, the difference falls to $0.2\%$.

We can summarise what we see in the computation:

  • Delta and gamma of the call. The plain calls pay their theta mostly for gamma exposure, at the break-even $v_0$, plus the carry of the delta hedge. A smaller part for their convexity in $u$, at the break-even $\xi$, closes the gap.
  • Accrual gamma for volswap. The volswap call pays a little over half of its theta for the accrual, at the same break-even $v_0$, and the rest for its convexity in $u$.
  • At the same $V_u$, the volswap call $V_{uu}$ is $127.7$ against $45.6$ for the plain calls, and its exposure to $\xi$ is about $8.5$ times larger.
  • Correlation. The value of the volswap call barely moves with the spot-volatility correlation: by less than $1\%$ between $\rho = -90\%$ and $\rho = 90\%$. The expected realised variance does not depend on $\rho$, and $\rho$ changes its distribution only slightly.

Conclusion and takeaways

We priced a plain call and a call on realised volatility in a one-factor stochastic volatility model, and computed their Greeks with a path-vectorised AAD tape. In the state variables of the model, spot, the accrued variance and the volatility factor $u$, the pricing equation balances the full theta to within $1$–$2\%$ and shows what theta pays for: gamma and the accrual at the break-even $v_0$, convexity in $u$ at the break-even $\xi$, and the cross term at $\rho v_0 \xi$.
